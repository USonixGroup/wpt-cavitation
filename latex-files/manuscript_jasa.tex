\begin{abstract}
	Acoustic emissions from cavitating bubbles contain complex information in the time and frequency domains. Passive cavitation detectors are used to measure these emissions. By processing this data, it becomes possible to extract cavitation features including broadband emissions, harmonics, sub- and ultra-harmonics. This allows monitoring cavitation which has diverse industrial applications. This paper presents a novel approach that utilizes the wavelet packet transform, statistical metrics and \km clustering to detect and classify these features in acoustic emissions. These findings enable us to quantitatively separate the stable and transient features of acoustic cavitation. This method is validated on synthetic acoustic emissions obtained by solving a single bubble dynamics (Gilmore-Akulichev model) and subsequently tested on experimental data from biomedical ultrasound applications.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Acoustic cavitation refers to the formation, growth and oscillation of bubbles (cavities filled with gas and vapour) in a liquid using acoustic waves. \cite{leighton1994} These bubbles may periodically oscillate whilst exposed to acoustic waves (known as stable cavitation) or grow and implode violently within a few cycles of excitation (known as inertial/transient cavitation). There are several physical effects associated with acoustic cavitation, including creation of free radicals, shock waves, shear stresses, micro-jets and micro-streaming in the insonated liquid.\cite{leighton1994} To utilize these effects for different applications, ultrasound waves are widely used to produce acoustic cavitation, e.g. imaging ultrasound,\cite{salgaonkar2009,averkiou2020} therapeutic ultrasound, \cite{kooiman2020,coussios2008,stride2019} sono-chemistry,\cite{bhangu2017,suslick1999} and sono-crystallization.\cite{haqshenas2016,haqshenas2018,lee2018,chow2003} To monitor these processes and to perform mechanistic studies, acoustic cavitation must be measured and evaluated quantitatively.

Passive\cite{coussios2008,gyongy2010} and active \cite{roy1990,cleveland2000} cavitation detections are the two main techniques used in biomedical ultrasound to quantitatively measure acoustic emissions generated by bubbles. Passive techniques use a single element ultrasound transducer, i.e. passive cavitation detector (PCD), to acquire acoustic emissions whereas active techniques use more sophisticated ultrasound array transducers. Passive methods are cost-effective and easy to implement, hence they have been widely used in basic and applied research. Some examples in biomedical ultrasound are as follows: hyperthermia and thermal ablation,\cite{kok2020} histotripsy,\cite{deandrade2021} drug and gene delivery,\cite{izadifar2019} blood-brain-barrier opening.\cite{timbie2015,mcdannold2006} Several methods have been developed to analyze acoustic emissions from cavitations, including spectral techniques, combination of spectral and digital filters and/or singular-value decomposition, time-frequency methods including short time-Fourier transform, Bilinear time-frequency distributions, and wavelet transforms.\cite{liang2006,frohly2000,coussios2008,farny2009,moholkar2002,chitnis2019} These works detect and classify the two main types of acoustic cavitations qualitatively. To carry out fundamental studies and elucidate the concomitant biological/physical effects produced by acoustic cavitation, advanced signal processing techniques are required to detect, extract, classify and quantitatively characterize the features of acoustic cavitation. To tackle this problem, we developed a method based discrete wavelet decomposition and spectral and statistical analyses to identify different types of cavitation and estimate their energies.\cite{haqshenas2015} Due to limitations of discrete wavelet transform, this method cannot isolate cavitation features in complex scenarios where we have overlapping spectral signatures. In this paper, we present a new method which uses combined discrete wavelet packet transform, statistical signal processing and \km clustering for quantitatively analyzing acoustic emissions, extracting and classifying cavitation features. The method is implemented in a Python library which is available in a GitHub repository.

The method is discussed in detail in Section \ref{sec:materials_methods}. For testing and verifying the accuracy and efficiency of the method, synthetic acoustic emissions were generated by solving the problem of single bubble dynamics using Gilmore-Akulichev model, presented in Section \ref{sec:GA_model}. The method was then applied to analyze the theoretical acoustic emissions. Finally, the method was applied to process experimental PCD data, explained in Section \ref{sec:analyzing_PCD_data}.

\subsection{Features of acoustic cavitation}
\label{sec:cavitation_physics}

The signal from a PCD may exhibit multiple spectral features, influenced by acoustic parameters and the physical properties of the liquid: (i) harmonics at frequencies of $nf$ where $f$ is the fundamental (excitation) frequency and $n$ is a positive integer, (ii) sub-harmonics at frequencies of $f/n$, (iii) ultra-harmonics at frequencies of $mf/n$ where $m > n$ is an integer that is not divisible by $n$, (iv) a broadband frequency component. The relationship between bubble dynamics and the these spectral features is complex, with various hypotheses proposed and studied in the literature. \cite{apfel1981,prosperetti1984, neppiras1980, lauterborn1976, lauterborn2010, lauterborn2015} A fundamental study of acoustic cavitation is beyond the scope of this work. However, a brief discussion is provided below to guide and support our analyses in the following sections. 

The fundamental (excitation) frequency $f$ is the main component observed when a bubble undergoes low-amplitude periodic oscillations or behaves as a scatterer, such as when the initial radii are larger than the incident wavelength. \cite{neppiras1980} In the stable cavitation regime, e.g. at low intensities, a bubble may begin to oscillate nonlinearly, due to the nonlinear coupling between free and forced pulsations, emitting high-order harmonics with the second harmonic $2f$ often to be the most prominent. The amplitude of the second harmonic is proportional to the square of the fundamental. \cite{neppiras1980} Theoretical studies show that for these nonlinear oscillations to occur, the ratio of the excitation frequency to the linear resonance frequency of the bubble, denoted by $f_r$, must be a rational fraction, i.e. $f/f_r = n/m$ and the pressure amplitude must be above a certain threshold. \cite{neppiras1980} The emissions from these nonlinear stable pulsations appear as sub-harmonics if $m=1$, meaning $f = nf_r$ and the bubbles are driven above their resonances, harmonics if $n=1$, meaning $f = f_r/m$, and ultra-harmonics otherwise (i.e., $n \neq m$), indicating that the bubbles are driven below their resonances. \cite{lauterborn1976, apfel1981, lauterborn2015} A bubble excited by a pulsed excitation with a small duty cycle and a large repetition period may also emit low-energy signals at the $f/2$ sub-harmonic. 

In the transient cavitation regime, at high intensities and above the transient cavitation threshold, the amplitudes of spectral features increase. The sub-harmonic signals are explained by the prolonged expansion phase that can occur when a bubble grows or oscillates over several cycles of sonication before collapsing. Additionally, a bubble with an initial size whose resonance frequency is close to sub-harmonics may resonate and undergo transient cavitation. The chaotic dynamics of transient bubbles \cite{lauterborn2015}, and shocks produced by inertial collapses manifest as broadband noise in the spectrum, which is often considered a hallmark of transient cavitation. \cite{frohly2000} In the case of a cloud of bubbles, the interaction between bubbles can produce harmonics and sub- and ultra-harmonics as well \cite{yasui2010}, further complicating the analysis of the PCD data. Moreover, emitted shockwaves can set neighboring bubbles into oscillations at their resonance frequencies which may appear as spectral contents unrelated to the excitation frequency $f$.

The spectral features of acoustic emissions exhibit distinct time-frequency structures depending on the cavitation regime. In stable cavitation, the (sub- and ultra-) harmonic components of the signal may persist over extended time intervals with localized frequency values corresponding to $mf/n$. In contrast, during transient cavitation, the emissions display impulsive temporal structures. These transient impulsive components can manifest in the frequency domain as both broadband noise and localized spectral components at $mf/n$.

The noise component (primarily measurement noise) exists over a long period and manifests as a broadband distribution in the frequency domain. To analyze these multi-resolution acoustic emissions, we utilize wavelet packet transform which will be discussed in the following section.

\subsection{Wavelet packet transform}\label{sec:WPT}

Wavelet packet bases were developed to effectively decompose a piecewise smooth signal with a varying time-frequency structure. Orthonormal wavelet packet bases divide the frequency axis into separate intervals of various sizes, known as scales. 
 
In fast orthogonal discrete wavelet transform (DWT), a bank of conjugate mirror filters and downsampling (by a factor of 2) are used to decompose a signal $x[n]$ of length $N=2^{n_0}$ into a cascade of coarser approximations $a_j[n]$ and finer details $d_j[n]$ at a scale $j$ \cite{mallat2009}

\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}  
		\label{eq:dwt-decomp}
		a_{j+1}[n] ~ & =~ \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} h[m]a_j[m-2n] \\
		d_{j+1}[n] ~ & =~ \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} g[m]a_j[m-2n] 
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}


\noindent where $a_0[n] = x[n]$, $h[m]$ and $g[m]$ are the impulse responses of the filters corresponding to the scaling and wavelet bases functions and are related as follows

\begin{align}  
	\label{eq:dwt-filters}   
	g[m]~=~(-1)^{1-m} h[1-m] 
\end{align}

\noindent $g[m]$ and $h[m]$ can be viewed as transfer functions of low- and high-pass filters, respectively, with length $M$. Thus, approximations and details represent the amplitudes of low- and high- frequency components, respectively, of the decomposed signal. In \eq{\ref{eq:dwt-decomp}}, the approximations ($a_j$) are only divided to low- and high-frequency components. Instead, we can iteratively split both approximation ($a_j$) and detail ($d_j$) at each scale, usually represented in a binary tree. This increases the computational complexity of discrete wavelet packet transform (DWPT) to $O(Nlog_2(N))$ compared to $O(N)$ for DWT.
\noindent Any node of the binary tree includes a packet of wavelet transformed component, i.e., the coefficients of filtered signal at a frequency subband, and is labeled by tuple $(j,k)$ where $j$ is the scale number (the depth of the node in the tree) and $k$ is the number of nodes that are on its left at the same scale (position of a node at a given depth). A schematic diagram of discrete wavelet packet transform using a bank of filters is shown in \figg{\ref{fig:dwpt_filter_bank}}.  

\begin{figure}
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=3in]{./figs/dwpt_filter_bank.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{The DWPT implemented as a filter bank}
	\label{fig:dwpt_filter_bank}
\end{figure}


The properties of the high- and low-pass filters are determined by the type and order of the wavelet function. There is a trade-off between the time and frequency resolution when selecting the order of the wavelet. A higher-order wavelet leads to subbands with less overlap, due to sharper cutoff and higher stopband attenuation, but it is computationally expensive. Here, the effectiveness of wavelets is assessed using the silhouette score in the clustering algorithm - further discussed in Section \ref{sec:k-means_intro}.

Compared with discrete wavelet transform, DWPT provides higher frequency resolution (and decreased time resolution) at higher frequencies. The more levels of transform, the narrower the frequency subbands. We will use Shannon entropy minimization algorithm \cite{coifman1992} to find the maximum scales of decomposition for each signal. In this algorithm, if the combined entropy of the child nodes is greater than that of the parent node, the nodes are deemed to have better information and are kept in the transform tree. Using this measure of entropy, the algorithm will tend to split strong harmonics further while containing transient features at higher-level nodes. This reduces the redundancy in wavelet packet transform, providing a more compact representation of the signal. It also addresses the class imbalance that comes from the excessive number of nodes at the higher frequencies in the full DWPT. This makes use of the primary strength of the DWPT in the flexibility it provides in tiling the time-frequency domain.	

The packets can be separately reconstructed into signals which combine to form the original signal. The stable and transient components of the acoustic emissions tend to have different time-frequency signatures, as discussed above, so the transform will help separate them out. Once separated, the packets can be classified to give an indication of the presence and extent of each type of cavitation.

\subsection{Statistical metrics for feature detection}
\label{sec:statistical_metrics}

To extract the signatures of inertial and stable cavitations at each subband, the DWPT coefficients of leaf nodes, i.e. nodes at the final (deepest) scale, are characterized by the following statistical metrics: energy, kurtosis, crest factor, skewness, entropy and the logarithms of energy. The energy of a packet is a measure of how much the signal is concentrated within that subband, calculated as follows

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:energy}                           
	E_{j,k} = \sum_{n=1}^{N_{j,k}} x_{j,k}[n]^2 
\end{align}

\noindent where $x_{j,k}[n]$ are coefficients in a subband $(j,k)$ and $N_{j,k}$ is the number of coefficients in the subband. $E_{0,0}$ is the total energy of the original signal. Note that using orthogonal wavelet bases for DWPT preserves the energy of the signal. Hence, the sum of the energy of all nodes in the final scale equals to the energy of the time series signal. The average energy of the coefficients in a node is defined as

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:avg_energy}                      
	\bar{E}_{j,k} = \dfrac{1}{N_{j,k}} E_{j,k} 
\end{align}

\noindent where $E_{j,k}$ is the energy of the coefficients in a subband $(j,k)$ and $N_{j,k}$ is the number of coefficients in the subband.

To better distinguish fundamental, sub- and ultra-harmonics, we also use the log-energy metric, i.e., $LE_{j,k}~=~ \log{E_{j,k}}$. To measure the energy distribution of a subband - compared to other subbands, we use Shannon entropy which is defined as

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:entropy}                                                                                   
	S_{j,k} = -\sum_{n=1}^{N_{j,k}} \dfrac{x_{j,k}[n]^2}{E_{j,k}} ~ \log{\dfrac{x_{j,k}[n]^2}{E_{j,k}}}. 
\end{align}

Level entropy is a variation on the calculation of entropy given by

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:level_entropy}                                                                             
	S'_{j,k} = -\sum_{n=1}^{N_{j,k}} \dfrac{x_{j,k}[n]^2}{E_{0,0}} ~ \log{\dfrac{x_{j,k}[n]^2}{E_{0,0}}} 
\end{align}

\noindent Level entropy is also used to obtain the minimum entropy decomposition for DWPT.

The spectral entropy is a measure of the complexity of the distribution in the spectrum of the subband. This calculated using \eq{\ref{eq:entropy}} where $x_{j,k}[n]$ is replaced with its magnitude spectrum. The lower the spectral entropy of a subband, the more deterministic components (information about harmonics) are within the subband. The transient components have a broadband frequency signature, hence a higher entropy can be attributed to transient and/or noise components of the signal. To distinguish these features we need additional statistical metrics.
%and $E_{j,k}$ is the energy of the magnitude spectrum.

Kurtosis is a measure of how often outliers occur in a signal. It is viewed as a metric for detecting transient cavitation. Kurtosis is calculated using the following relationship

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:kurtosis}                                                                        
	K_{j,k} = \dfrac{1}{n\sigma_{j,k}^4} \sum_{n=1}^{N_{j,k}}(x_{j,k}[n]-\overline{x}_{j,k})^4 
\end{align}

\noindent where $\overline{x}_{j,k}$ and $\sigma_{j,k}$ are the mean and standard deviation of the coefficients of the node $(j,k)$.

Skewness measures the extent to which a distribution is asymmetrical. It is given by

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:skewness}                                                                         
	Sk_{j,k} = \dfrac{1}{n\sigma_{j,k}^3} \sum_{n=1}^{N_{j,k}}(x_{j,k}[n]-\overline{x}_{j,k})^3 
\end{align}

\noindent The absolute value of the skewness of the coefficients is expected to be larger in magnitude when the signal has transient components.

The crest factor is the ratio between the peak value and the mean of the signal, indicating how extreme the peaks are in the signal. It is obtained by

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:crest_factor}                                                       
	C_{j,k} = \dfrac{{\max{(\left\lvert x_{j,k}[n] \right\rvert )}}^2}{x_{rms}^2} 
\end{align}
\noindent where $x_{rms}$ is the root-mean-square of $x_{j,k}[n]$.

A feature matrix of these statistical metrics of all subbands is formed. The next step of the algorithm is to classify these features, hence subbands. A commonly used technique, adopted in our algorithm too, is \km clustering, \cite{yiakopoulos2011, shete2013} which is discussed in the next section. 

\subsection{\km clustering for feature classification}
\label{sec:k-means_intro}
\km splits a dataset comprised of $n$ observations, each with a number of features, into $k$ clusters. The aim of the algorithm is to minimize the total loss measured as the sum of the Euclidean distances between the centroid of the cluster and each point assigned to it (see \eq{\ref{eq:kmeans}}). To do this, the cluster centroids are initiated randomly and each data point is assigned to the nearest cluster. The clusters are iteratively re-allocated to minimize the loss function.

\begin{align}
	\Theta = \sum_{i=1}^{n}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\rho^{(i,j)} \lVert x^{(i)} - \mu^{(j)} \rVert^2}} 
	\label{eq:kmeans}                                                                         
\end{align}

\noindent where $\Theta$ is the distortion (loss) function, $\rho$ is the assignment of the data point $i$ to cluster $j$, and $x$ and $\mu$ are the data point and cluster centroid respectively. \km is a non-convex optimization problem and yields different results depending on the starting position of the clusters. This is further discussed in section \ref{sec:method_overview}.

Another issue of the \km algorithm is that the number of clusters $k$ must be specified before training. This presents a challenge in this scenario where the number of clusters is expected to vary depending on the type of cavitation that occurs. For example, a signal comprised of a tone and noise, representing a measurement of stable cavitation, results in nodes which fall into two clusters: those representing noise, and those representing the fundamental frequency (harmonic component). Once harmonics and transients are added to the signal, more clusters could be formed. Therefore, the algorithm must adapt to the number of clusters that exist in the data. Several techniques have been developed for choosing the optimal number of clusters. \cite{schubert2022stop, yuan2019research} Here, we will use the silhouette score to to automate the selection of $k$. The silhouette score is a measure of how similar an object is to its own cluster (cohesion) compared to other clusters (separation), ranging from -1 to 1, where a high value indicates that the object is well matched to its own cluster and poorly matched to neighboring clusters. The silhouette score is easy to implement and works well for small datasets. \cite{shahapure2020cluster} It is given by

\begin{align}
	s = \frac{b-a}{\max(a,b)}\label{eq:silhouette_score} 
\end{align}

\noindent where $b$ is the mean distance between a point and the points in the nearest cluster $a$ is the mean distance between the point and all other points in the same cluster. The nearest cluster is found by finding the cluster centre that is closest the the point. The metric used to select $k$ is the average silhouette score for every point in the dataset. We will iteratively change $k$ to maximize the silhouette score.



% The combination of \km and DWPT has been previously applied to identify and classify the transients and harmonics in complex signals. Yiakopulos et al. \cite{yiakopoulos2011} applied the \km algorithm to the problem of fault detection for rolling element bearings. Their method used two instances of \km, one to determine whether the bearing was faulty and another to determine the nature of the fault. Each algorithm used a different set of used a different of features. They noticed that the non-convexity of \km was a major disadvantage of the \km algorithm and identified several techniques that have been developed to reduce the effect of this. In their method, the centres were initialised using results from a dynamic model of the system. Shete et al. \cite{shete2013} also used the \km algorithm to detect the k-complex in EEG signals. This was achieved by using the \km algorithm to compare feature of a reference k-complex signal and extracted sections of an input signal to determine whether that section contained a k-complex. This allows the training of the algorithm by providing it with examples of the feature that is sought after. Desai et al. \cite{desai2020} combined the WPT with \km to categorise EEG data into different stress states. They calculated statistical features from the reconstructed coefficients at specific subbands and used these as features in the \km algorithm. This study computes statistical features based on the wavelet coefficients themselves which avoids the computational load introduced by the inverse WPT. This does not cause a drop in performance.

\subsection{Method overview}
\label{sec:method_overview}
The proposed algorithm is outlined in Fig. \ref{fig:method_flowchart}. The PCD data are normalized by taking off the mean and dividing by the standard deviation. Next, the DWPT is applied to the signal, decomposing it into frequency subbands using the minimum entropy decomposition algorithm and the statistical metrics are calculated at the leaf nodes. The nodes with frequency higher than the PCD's cutoff are excluded. These metrics are normalized using a standard scalar, fitting each set of statistical metrics to a standard Gaussian distribution to ensure that their difference in scale does not affect clustering. Each node, now represented as a set of features calculated from their coefficients, is fed into the \km algorithm to be clustered. We only need to specify the wavelet family and the order of the wavelet function. The depth of decomposition and the number of clusters are determined automatically.

\subsection{\km parameters}
The k-means algorithm was run with Lloyd's algorithm described in Section \ref{sec:k-means_intro}. The clustering problem is sparse, so the effects the non-convexity of the k-means algorithm is mitigated using multiple runs of the k-means algorithm. The clustering with the best value of the distortion function (see \eq{\ref{eq:kmeans}}) is selected. Furthermore, the k-means++ algorithm \cite{arthur2007} is used to select the starting centroids. This method selects data points as clusters one-by-one, while enforcing that points further away from existing selections are more likely to be chosen. This can improve clustering time and performance, reducing the chance that clusters are merged.
The algorithm is implemented as a Python open-source library, \cite{wpt-cavitation2024} and validated using synthetic acoustic emissions generated by numerically solving Gilmore--Akulichev equations. Its performance is then tested and verified on experimental PCD data. 

\subsection{Gilmore--Akulichev model}
\label{sec:GA_model}
Gilmore's equation describes the dynamics of a spherical bubble in a compressible liquid, undergoing symmetric oscillations due to acoustic excitations. The model has been validated against experimental data and widely used for studying acoustic cavitation.\cite{lauterborn2010} The Gilmore equation is expressed as follows \cite{gilmore1952}

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:gilmore}
	  & R \ddot{R}\left(1~-~\dfrac{\dot{R}}{C}\right) ~+~ \dfrac{3}{2}{\dot{R}}^2 \left(1~-~\dfrac{\dot{R}}{3C}\right) ~=~ \nonumber \\
	  & H \left(1~+~\dfrac{\dot{R}}{C}\right)  ~+~ \dfrac{R \dot{H}}{C}\left(1~-~\dfrac{\dot{R}}{C}\right)                           
\end{align}

\noindent where $R$ is the instantaneous bubble radius, $C$ and $H$ are the speed of sound in the liquid at the bubble wall, and the enthalpy difference in the liquid at the bubble wall (at pressure $P(R)$) and at infinity (at $P_\infty$), respectively. $P_\infty$ is the sum of the static reference pressure $P_0$ and the acoustic pressure $P_{ac}(t)$, i.e., $P_\infty(t)=P_0~+~P_{ac}(t)$. Time derivatives are designated by dots. Gilmore's model implicitly assumes that the acoustic pressure is applied instantaneously and uniformly around the bubble without specifying a source location in the liquid. Thus, the liquid density and speed of sound at infinity are taken at the static reference state, denoted by $\rho_0$ and $c_0$, respectively, below. $C$ and $H$ are calculated using the following equations

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:wavespeed}        
	C ~=~ \sqrt{c_0^2~+~(n-1)H} 
\end{align}

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:enthalpy}
	H ~=~ \dfrac{n A^{1/n}}{(n-1)\rho_0} & \left[ \left(P(R) ~+~ B\right)^{\left(1-1/n\right)} ~ - \right. \nonumber \\
	                                     & \left. \left(P_\infty(t) ~+~ B\right)^{\left(1-1/n\right)} \right]        
\end{align}

\noindent where $A, B,~\mathrm{and}~n$ are constants in the Tait's equation of state which are obtained experimentally. The time derivative of the enthalpy in the liquid at the bubble wall becomes

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:enthalpy_dot}
	\dot{H} ~=~ \dfrac{A^{1/n}}{\rho_0} & \left[ \dot{P}(R)\left(P(R) ~+~ B\right)^{-1/n} ~ - \right. \nonumber \\
	                                    & \left. \dot{P}_{ac}(t) \left(P_\infty(t) ~+~ B\right)^{-1/n} \right]. 
\end{align}

\noindent Note that $\dot{P}_\infty(t)=\dot{P}_{ac}(t)$. $P(R)$ is calculated using a modified Laplace equation

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:pressure_at_R}                                                                                                            
	P(R)~=~ \left(P_0~+~\frac{2\sigma}{R_0}\right) \left(\dfrac{R_0}{R}\right)^{3\gamma}~-~\dfrac{2\sigma}{R}~-~\dfrac{4\mu \dot{R}}{R} 
\end{align}

\noindent where $R_0$ is the initial bubble radius, $\gamma$, $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the adiabatic index (ratio of specific heats), dynamic viscosity, and surface tension, respectively. Finally, taking the time derivative of the above equation, we arrive at

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:pressure_at_R_dot}
	\dot{P}(R)~=~ & -3\gamma\dfrac{\dot{R}}{R} \left(P_0~+~\dfrac{2\sigma}{R_0}\right) \left(\dfrac{R_0}{R}\right)^{3\gamma}~+~ \nonumber \\
	              & \dfrac{\dot{R}}{R}\dfrac{2\sigma}{R}~+~4\mu \left(\dfrac{\dot{R}}{R}\right)^2~-~\dfrac{4\mu \ddot{R}}{R}.             
\end{align}

\noindent Eqs. \ref{eq:wavespeed}-\ref{eq:pressure_at_R_dot} are used to numerically solve the Gilmore's equation \eq{\ref{eq:gilmore}}. The radiated pressure in the liquid is then given by the Akulichev formula

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:akulichev}
	P_{r}(t)= & A \left(\dfrac{2}{n+1} ~ + ~\dfrac{n-1}{n+1} \left( 1 + \dfrac{n+1}{r c_0^2 } G \right)^{1/2} \right)^{2n/(n-1)} \nonumber \\
	          & ~ - ~ B                                                                                                                    
\end{align}

\noindent where $r$ is the distance from the centre of the bubble, and $G=R(H ~+~ 0.5\dot{R}^2)$. This equation assumes that acoustic emissions from an oscillating bubble propagate as outward traveling spherical waves. The Gilmore--Akulichev (GA) model is nondimensionalized and solved numerically using a stiff ordinary differential equation solver for different sonication conditions. The implementation is included in the GitHub repository. \cite{wpt-cavitation2024} The implementation and numerical results were verified against published results for identical parameters. White Gaussian noise (WGN) with the signal-to-noise ratio of \SI{30}{\dB} was added to the calculated pressures to simulate the measurement noise.

This section presents the results of studies aiming to test the algorithm's ability for identifying and classifying the features of stable and transient cavitation. \subsection{Feature Selection}
\label{sec:feature_selection}
%explain how self-organisisng maps work?
The features space is explored using self-organising maps, \cite{kohonen2012} a type of neural network which can be used to visualise high dimensional data in two dimensions. The algorithm generates nodes which are arranged in a two-dimensional space, each containing an array of weights, one for each input feature. The nodes can be thought of as cluster centres which are interconnected such that neighbouring neurons have similar features. The features can be plotted separately, giving a set of maps which can be visually compared to evaluate the usefulness of each feature. The details of the algorithm will not be discussed here and can be found in Haroun et al. \cite{haroun2017feature}, used in the context of bearing fault detection and diagnosis. 

The data utilized to train the self-organising map uses 192 signals generated at a sampling rate of \SI{50}{\mega\hertz}, representing both stable and transient cavitation. The signals have noise added to them and are windowed and normalised as described in \figg{\ref{fig:method_flowchart}}. The results are shown in Fig. \ref{fig:som}. When the maps are visually similar, their respective features are correlated with each other. This shows that the high energy DWPT nodes tend to have low skewness, kurtosis, and spectral entropy, as expected given the physical interpretations of each. This area represents packet nodes containing fundamental frequencies. The spectral entropy and level entropy are able to find nodes around the high energy area. These represent harmonics which are not easily picked out using average energy alone. The nodes with high frequency and kurtosis, and low energy indicate signals with sharp transients. The rest of the high-frequency nodes represent noise.

Several plots look similar to each other, indicating redundancy in the feature space. Skewness, kurtosis, and crest factor perform similarly, while level entropy (normalised by the energy of the original signal), and spectral entropy are highly correlated. Overall, the best features are hand picked as average energy, kurtosis, log average energy, and spectral entropy and used for identifying nodes representing fundamentals, harmonics, and noise.

\subsection{Bubble dynamics data}
\label{sec:GA_data}
The temporal description of the incident ultrasound pressure is modelled as a sine wave, i.e., $P_{ac}(t)= P_A sin(2 \pi f t)$ where $P_A$ and $f$ are the amplitude and frequency of the ultrasound wave. The frequency and pulse length are set to \SI{1.1}{\mega\hertz} and $100/f$, to mimic experiments, and $P_A=1 \si{\mega\pascal}$ for the results presented below. We choose water and oxygen for the liquid and the gas. The physical parameters used for numerical simulations are as follows (at room temperature \SI{293}{\kelvin} and atmospheric pressure $p_0=100 \si{\kilo\pascal}$): $\rho_0=\SI{e3}{\kilogram\per\cubic\metre}$, $\sigma=\SI{7.25e-2}{\newton\per\metre}$, $\mu=\SI{e-3}{\pascal\second}$, $c_0=\SI{1482}{\metre\per\second}$, $n=7$, $A=\SI{3.04e2}{\mega\pascal}$, $B=\SI{3.039e2}{\mega\pascal}$ and $\gamma=1.4$. The initial conditions are: $R(0)=R_0$ and $\dot{R}(0)=0$. The radius of a resonant bubble at the driving frequency (\SI{1.1}{\mega\hertz}) is \SI{3.4}{\micro\metre} - calculated using the linear resonance equation.\cite{neppiras1980} Here, three scenarios are simulated in which the initial radii are (i) much smaller than the resonant bubble, i.e. $R_0=\SI{0.5}{\micro\metre}$, (ii) close to the size of the resonant bubble; $R_0=\SI{5}{\micro\metre}$, and (iii) much larger than the resonant bubble, i.e., $R_0=\SI{50}{\micro\metre}$, resulting in transient and stable cavitation regimes.  

The radiated pressures at $r=\SI{100}{\micro\metre}$ and their spectra are shown in Figs XX. To analyze the emissions, the Daubechies wavelet function of order 20 was chosen in accordance with the physics of cavitation and the impulsive nature of desired transient component in transient cavitation. The depth of the decomposition was determined for each signal using the minimum Shannon entropy criterion, discussed before. The choice of the wavelet function is not unique. Our numerical studies (not shown here) confirmed that other orthonormal wavelet functions with relatively high order (e.g., Coiflet10, Symlet20, etc.) can effectively extract cavitation signatures in the signal. Statistical metrics defined in Section \ref{sec:statistical_metrics} were calculated for all wavelet packet leaves. Analyzing the feature space, we selected a smaller subset of features (i.e., spectral entropy, log average energy, kurtosis, and average energy) for clustering and classification of the packets - discussed in detail in Section \ref{sec:feature_selection}. Initially, the \km clustering is enforced to classify the leaves into two clusters, separating transient from periodic components. Figures XX and YY depict the reconstructed components and their spectra. The values of the classifying metrics calculated at the centers of these clusters are shown in bar charts in Fig. XX.

The algorithm is once again able to distinguish between the fundamental frequency, low frequency noise, and high frequency noise but is unable to separate the harmonics and sub-harmonics from the noise. This is because the harmonics and other low-frequency components share similar log average energy. Removing log average energy metric from classification, results in a better separation between the harmonics and fundamental, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:280_1_fft_alt}. However, this combines measurement noise and broadband components in a single cluster, mislabeling the corresponding packets. The algorithm is consistently able to extract and categorise the fundamental frequency of the signal. However, it struggles with separating harmonics, broadband noise, and measurement noise.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presents a novel approach developed for analyzing PCD data by clustering DWPT nodes, in an attempt to create an automated algorithm that can separate out features relating to stable and transient cavitation. The DWPT has been found to be useful in separating out these components in a flexible manner, especially when combined with the minimum entropy decomposition, which is able to generate a detailed time-frequency tiling quickly. Several features were assessed to help classify the nodes. Self-organising maps were used to help visualise the feature space, a useful method in determining which ones may be the most useful. However, the algorithm is unable to consistently separate harmonics and sub-harmonics from lower-kurtosis noise. This leads to several suggestions for future work:
	
\begin{itemize}
	\item Explore alternative algorithms to \km which may be able to find more meaningful clusters.
	\item Explore other methods of generating training data with more involved models.
	\item Expand the work to cavitation in other common mediums like tissue.
	\item Separate noise generated through cavitation and measurement noise.
	\item Extend the algorithm to automatically detect cavitation activity over noise. This can be useful for applications where the ultrasound is pulsed.
	\item Explore the use of entropy, getting around the issue discussed in section \ref{sec:feature_selection} where the level of the transform dominates their measure. Possible approaches could involve calculating the entropy and spectral entropy on the reconstructed signals which always have the same length as the original signal or by using an undecimated transform.
	\item The sparseness of the algorithm could be addressed by clustering nodes from more than one sample. This could allow it to classify harmonics in separate clusters.
\end{itemize}
